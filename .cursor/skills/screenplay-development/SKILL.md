# Screenplay Development

## Purpose

Turn an idea into a complete, compelling, filmable feature screenplay. Treat the methods in this skill as diagnostic instruments, not laws. Causality and character choice outrank page counts and beat labels.

## Core standard

A screenplay is ready only when:

1. A specific protagonist actively pursues a visible goal.
2. Major turns arise from choices and consequences, not authorial rescue.
3. The antagonist can alter the situation and cannot simply leave.
4. External conflict exposes an internal survival strategy.
5. Pressure changes in kind as well as degree.
6. Every retained scene changes value, relationship, knowledge, or power.
7. Dialogue acts on another person and contains more than literal information.
8. The page contains only what can be seen, heard, or performed.
9. The climax makes the old solution impossible and demands a consequential choice.
10. The resolution closes the event; the ending shows how the character will now live.

## Interaction contract

Determine the working mode first:

- **Develop**: build from a raw idea.
- **Outline**: turn an established story engine into beats and scenes.
- **Draft**: write screenplay pages from an approved scene plan.
- **Diagnose**: identify the highest-level failure in existing material.
- **Rewrite**: repair one named problem across the whole screenplay.

Do not jump from a raw idea to polished scenes. Agree on the story engine and ending before detailed outlining. If the user asks for autonomous development, state necessary assumptions and proceed.

Ask only questions whose answers materially change the story. Prefer one focused decision at a time. When presenting alternatives, explain what each version makes the movie about.

## Workflow

Track the active phase and do not advance past a failed gate.

### Phase 1: Qualify the idea

Reduce the idea to:

> A specific person, in a specific situation, must do a specific thing.

Check:

- Why does the story begin now?
- Why must this be shown as a movie rather than explained as an essay?
- What recurring visual situation does the premise promise?
- What human pressure makes the idea last for a feature?

**Gate:** The idea contains a person taking action, not only a world, issue, mood, or twist.

### Phase 2: Define the story engine

Create three distinct statements:

1. **Logline**: protagonist + active goal + main opposition + stakes + defining contrast.
2. **Premise**: a character force, tested through conflict, causes a result.
3. **Theme question**: an arguable question the characters answer differently.

Keep them separate. The logline says what story this is; the premise states the causal claim; the theme question organizes competing attitudes.

Identify the genre promise:

- What experience must the audience receive?
- Which familiar devices are exhausted?
- What changes in character, setting, cost, or solution make this version distinct?

Use the Story Engine template in [templates.md](templates.md).

**Gate:** A new listener understands the movie without supplementary lore and cannot predict the entire outcome.

### Phase 3: Design the ending before the opening

Write:

- **Event resolution**: what finally happens.
- **Character ending**: who the protagonist becomes and how life will continue.
- **Climactic choice**: the action that produces both.
- **Final image**: visible evidence of change.

Choose the opening only after the ending is known. Enter when the protagonist must decide and the main conflict is about to become active. Start later and end earlier than instinct suggests.

**Gate:** The ending is caused by prior choices and cannot be replaced by luck, rescue, or a speech.

### Phase 4: Choose and build the protagonist

Choose the person with:

- the most urgent desire;
- the greatest personal loss;
- the most intimate connection to the opposition;
- the largest necessary change;
- the power to make the climactic choice.

Build the character engine:

- visible want;
- unacknowledged need;
- feared verdict about the self;
- habitual defense or compensation;
- strength and flaw produced by the same strategy;
- concrete physiological, social, and psychological causes;
- action that creates immediate audience access.

Psychological theories are hypotheses, never diagnoses. Retain an explanation only if it predicts choices and visible consequences.

Use the Character Engine template in [templates.md](templates.md).

**Gate:** The same character logic explains competence, recurring mistakes, resistance to change, and the climax.

### Phase 5: Build the opposition and relationship system

For protagonist and antagonist, define:

- incompatible desired outcomes;
- credible reasons;
- means to change the situation;
- the bond that prevents either side from simply leaving;
- the fear, need, or strategy they share;
- what the antagonist embodies that the protagonist refuses to admit.

Give every major supporting character a relationship function: guide, threshold, temptation, witness, alternative, or source of missing capacity. A role may change function during the story.

Design the secondary line to alter the final solution. Trust and intimacy must be earned through escalating acts of vulnerable disclosure and response.

**Gate:** Removing the antagonist or secondary line would materially change the protagonist's decisions and the climax.

### Phase 6: Build the causal spine

Use these functions, not mandatory page numbers:

1. Opening state.
2. Disruption.
3. Debate and cost of action.
4. Protagonist chooses the new world.
5. Actions meet counteractions.
6. Midpoint changes the kind of pressure.
7. External opposition and internal fracture intensify.
8. The old solution fails completely.
9. Main-line experience and relationship insight form a new solution.
10. Climactic choice.
11. Event resolution.
12. Character ending and final image.

For each turn, write:

> Because the character did X, Y became unavoidable; therefore the character now does Z.

If only “and then” connects two turns, the causal spine is broken.

**Gate:** Each major turn changes direction or pressure and follows from prior action.

### Phase 7: Expand the spine into cards

Use one card per major dramatic action. Approximately forty cards can diagnose a commercial feature, but are not a quota and do not equal forty scenes.

Each card records:

- location and time;
- present characters;
- point-of-view character;
- immediate objective;
- obstacle;
- tactic and counteraction;
- turn;
- before/after value;
- causal link to the next card;
- story line served.

Arrange cards by act or movement. Look for repetition, missing escalation, absent characters, abandoned secondary lines, and an empty final movement. Blank cards are allowed when the function is known but the execution is not.

**Gate:** The cards can be told aloud as one continuous chain and every movement reaches a clear change.

### Phase 8: Design each scene

Before drafting, complete the Scene Specification in [templates.md](templates.md).

A scene must:

- enter shortly before the conflict becomes active;
- give each important character an objective;
- proceed through action and reaction;
- force at least one tactic change;
- alter value, relationship, knowledge, or power;
- expose character while advancing plot;
- leave after the consequence is clear.

Put exposition inside struggle. Make information a weapon, lure, defense, demand, or reluctant concession. Use setting and physical activity to carry conflict; do not add business that competes with essential information.

**Gate:** The writer can state in one sentence what is different at the end. If nothing changes, merge, redesign, or cut the scene.

### Phase 9: Write dialogue as action

For every exchange, identify what the speaker is doing to the listener: testing, seducing, calming, cornering, delaying, misleading, begging, refusing, or another directed action.

Change the tactic when the response fails. A synonym is not a new beat.

Use:

- subtext: literal words differ from the pursued result;
- a third object: characters discuss one thing while contesting another;
- omission and silence;
- vocabulary and syntax grown from profession, class, knowledge, history, and immediate pressure.

Read dialogue aloud. Cut greetings, agreement, duplicated information, explanatory emotion, and lines that an action can perform better.

**Gate:** Removing a line removes an action, not merely information.

### Phase 10: Draft filmable pages

Write only what can be seen, heard, or performed:

- scene heading;
- visible subject and action;
- necessary sound;
- character name and dialogue.

Do not write unfilmable psychology, literary explanation, shot-by-shot direction, or decorative biography. Let behavior reveal the research done off the page.

Preserve forward motion in the first draft:

- use a fixed writing schedule;
- do not polish from page one while the draft is incomplete;
- record unrelated new ideas and return to this story;
- follow a better local discovery;
- if a discovery changes goal, turn, or ending, update the outline before continuing;
- finish even when some scenes are provisional.

**Gate:** A complete draft reaches the designed ending and can be read without oral explanation.

### Phase 11: Rewrite by passes

Save a version before each pass. Do not repair every layer at once.

Run passes in this order:

1. Story engine: logline, premise, theme question.
2. Causality: choices, consequences, coincidences, ending.
3. Protagonist: want, need, defense, agency, change.
4. Opposition: power, reason, binding relationship, escalation.
5. Structure: entry, turns, midpoint, old-solution failure, climax.
6. Relationships: secondary line, vulnerability, return to the main line.
7. Scenes: purpose, turn, before/after value, entry and exit.
8. Exposition and visual storytelling.
9. Dialogue action, subtext, compression, voice.
10. Pace and allocation of screen time.
11. Page craft, continuity, format, spelling.

For diagnosis, begin at the highest failing layer. Do not polish dialogue in a scene likely to be removed.

## Final audit

Use [reference.md](reference.md) for the full diagnostic questions. At minimum, verify:

- The title and logline promise the movie that was written.
- The protagonist causes the main turns.
- Opposition grows through action and counteraction.
- The midpoint changes pressure rather than marking elapsed time.
- The lowest point invalidates the psychological as well as practical old solution.
- The climax resolves conflict through a costly choice.
- The secondary line changes the final method.
- Every scene turns.
- Every retained detail advances plot and reveals character.
- Dialogue is playable action with subtext.
- The final image proves change without explaining it.

## Output behavior

Match output to the active phase. Do not flood the user with later-stage material before earlier decisions are stable.

When delivering development work:

1. Lead with the strongest judgment.
2. Separate established decisions from assumptions.
3. Show the current artifact.
4. State the failed or passed gate.
5. Ask for the single decision needed to continue, unless autonomous development was requested.

When the user asks for a video-generation prompt, finish or confirm the thesis and story spine first, then use the model-specific Seedance or MiniMax H3 skill. Do not put generation-prompt syntax into screenplay pages.
